William Colenso (1811–1899) Early scientific exploration and botanical collecting in the Ruahine and Kaweka ranges.

William Colenso was the first person to systematically document the flora of much of the central and southern North Island interior, including areas that would later become the Ruahine, Kaweka and Kaimanawa Forest Parks. Between the 1840s and 1880s he undertook arduous journeys across the interior, often travelling on foot through country that was then largely unknown to European science. During these expeditions he collected thousands of plant specimens, discovered numerous new species, and produced detailed observations on forest composition, alpine vegetation, wetlands and river valleys. His accounts provide some of the earliest descriptions of the condition of the Ruahine and Kaweka forests before widespread European settlement, burning and the introduction of browsing mammals. Although Colenso worked before the emergence of ecology as a scientific discipline, his meticulous field notes, specimen collections and publications established a baseline against which later botanists such as Leonard Cockayne, Norman Elder and A. Cunningham could assess ecological change. For studies of long-term vegetation history in the North Island ranges, Colenso's records remain invaluable because they document the landscape in a largely pre-modification state.

Leonard Cockayne (1855–1934)	Established New Zealand plant ecology and vegetation science.

Leonard Cockayne laid the scientific foundations for understanding the vegetation of New Zealand's mountain ranges, including the Aorangi, Rimutaka, Tararua, Ruahine, Kaweka and Kaimanawa regions. Through extensive fieldwork and his landmark synthesis, The Vegetation of New Zealand, he established the framework of plant ecology in New Zealand, describing alpine, subalpine, forest and shrubland communities in relation to climate, soils and geography. Cockayne's concepts of plant succession, ecological adaptation, vegetation zones and botanical provinces shaped subsequent research in the North Island ranges. Although he did not undertake the detailed local surveys that later botanists would conduct, virtually all later ecological work in these ranges built upon the classifications, terminology and ecological interpretations that he developed.

Norman Lascelles Elder (1896–1974)	Produced the classic vegetation surveys of Tararua, Ruahine, Kaweka and Kaimanawa ranges.
Norman L. Elder was the principal field botanist and ecological chronicler of the central North Island ranges during the mid-twentieth century. Beginning with systematic botanical surveys of the Tararua Range in the 1930s, he produced one of the first comprehensive accounts of its flora and vegetation. After moving to Hawke's Bay, he extended his investigations northward through the Ruahine, Kaweka and Kaimanawa ranges, documenting vegetation patterns, forest composition, alpine communities and the effects of fire, grazing and human occupation. His 1965 paper Vegetation of the Ruahine Range: An Introduction synthesised three decades of observation and portrayed the Ruahines as a crucial ecological transition zone linking the volcanic plateau with the southern North Island mountains. Elder's work provided the baseline descriptions upon which later Forest Service and conservation research was built.

Mavis Melville Davidson (1910–2004)	Demonstrated ecological impacts of deer on mountain forests.

Although trained as a zoologist rather than a botanist, Mavis Davidson made a major contribution to understanding vegetation change in the Tararua, Kaweka and Kaimanawa ranges through her pioneering studies of deer ecology. Working for the New Zealand Forest Service, she examined how red deer and later sika deer altered indigenous forests and subalpine vegetation. Her influential study of red deer in the Tararua Mountains demonstrated that browsing pressure was transforming forest composition and creating new ecological trajectories distinct from pre-European vegetation. She subsequently became one of New Zealand's leading authorities on sika deer in the Kaweka and Kaimanawa ranges, documenting the interactions between ungulates, forest regeneration and mountain ecosystems. Davidson's work helped establish the scientific basis for modern deer-management policies in North Island mountain forests.

Anthony (Tony) Druce (1920–1999)  Comprehensive floristic documentation and field botany of North Island mountain systems.
 One of the twentieth century's foremost field botanist of the North Island mountains. Over five decades he conducted intensive botanical exploration throughout the Tararua, Rimutaka, Ruahine, Kaweka and Kaimanawa ranges, compiling extraordinarily comprehensive species lists, collecting tens of thousands of specimens, and documenting vegetation patterns from lowland forest to alpine herbfield. His early publications included accounts of the Kaimanawas, and he became a principal authority on North Island mountain vegetation. Elder explicitly acknowledged Druce as a collaborator in the fieldwork underlying Vegetation of the Ruahine Range: An Introduction (1965).

Druce's importance lay not only in discovery but in documentation. He maintained detailed plant lists for hundreds of localities, including major North Island mountain systems, and continually revised them as taxonomy and field knowledge advanced. His unpublished checklists for the Tararua and Rimutaka ranges became standard references for botanists and conservation managers.

The Kaimanawas were especially important in his career. One of his earliest papers was A Botanical Trip to the Northern Kaimanawas (1949), and he continued collecting there throughout his life. Many later taxonomic studies relied heavily on specimens he collected from the Kaimanawa, Kaweka, Ruahine and Tararua ranges.

Alick Lindsay Poole (1922–2000)   co-author of Trees and Shrubs of New Zealand is best known as a New Zealand botanist and forester, and his work had a significant influence on understanding and managing native vegetation and the ecological pressures affecting it. One of his key contributions was co-authoring Trees and Shrubs of New Zealand, a foundational reference work for identifying native species and guiding ecological restoration and planting decisions. In relation to wild animal control, Poole’s broader forestry leadership helped shape official recognition of how introduced browsing animals such as deer and possums were severely limiting regeneration of native forests. During his time in senior roles within the New Zealand Forest Service, he supported and advanced research and management approaches that led to more systematic deer control programmes, especially in upland forest parks. These programmes were particularly important in regions such as the Tararua, Ruahine, Kaweka, Kaimanawa, Rimutaka (now Remutaka), and Aorangi Forest Parks, where heavy deer browsing had caused widespread damage to understory vegetation and slowed forest recovery. Poole’s work helped establish the scientific and policy basis for integrated wild animal control across these connected mountain ranges, linking forestry science with on-the-ground pest management aimed at restoring native tree and shrub communities.

Alastair (Lindsay) Poole began his career in the New Zealand Forest Service (NZFS) in the 1920s as a trainee at the Whakarewarewa nursery, later returning after university and field experience to hold increasingly senior technical and administrative roles. He ultimately rose through the organisation to become Director-General of the NZ Forest Service (1961–1971), where he helped shape national forestry policy, research, and large-scale planting and conservation programmes.

Ash Cunningham

A. Cunningham's major contribution to the ecology of the North Island ranges was documenting how mountain forests changed under the combined influences of land clearance, deer, possums, goats, erosion and climatic disturbance. His landmark paper A Century of Change in the Forests of the Ruahine Range, North Island, New Zealand: 1870–1970 reconstructed a hundred years of ecological transformation by synthesising historical accounts, botanical observations and Forest Service records. He showed that the Ruahine forests described by early travellers such as William Colenso exhibited little evidence of widespread decline before European settlement, whereas by the mid-twentieth century browsing mammals and possums had profoundly altered forest structure, reduced understorey vegetation, and contributed to canopy collapse in many areas. Cunningham's work shifted attention from describing vegetation patterns to understanding ecological processes and long-term change, providing an essential bridge between Elder's descriptive vegetation surveys and the modern era of ecosystem monitoring and conservation management in the Ruahine, Kaweka and neighbouring ranges. He later expanded this perspective through broader catchment and management studies, including The Ruahine Range (with P. W. Stribling), which integrated vegetation, erosion and land-use issues at the scale of an entire mountain system.


Shaun Barnett (1969–2024)
Over more than three decades, Shaun Barnett made an exceptional contribution to understanding and appreciating the wild mountain landscapes of the lower and central North Island. Through his books, magazine articles, photography and historical research, he documented the character, ecology, routes, huts and human stories of the Aorangi Forest Park, Remutaka Forest Park, Tararua Forest Park, Ruahine Forest Park, Kaweka Forest Park and Kaimanawa Forest Park. Barnett combined the eye of a naturalist, the endurance of a dedicated tramper and the skill of a historian, helping generations of New Zealanders understand these ranges not simply as recreational destinations but as interconnected wilderness landscapes shaped by geology, conservation, backcountry culture and Māori and European histories. His writing and photography brought particular attention to the often-overlooked axial ranges of the North Island, recording their remote huts, rugged tops and distinctive wilderness values, and inspiring a deeper public appreciation of some of Aotearoa’s most important backcountry environments.
